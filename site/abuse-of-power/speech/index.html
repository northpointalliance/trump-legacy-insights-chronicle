Speech, TDS & the First Amendment

How “Trump Derangement Syndrome” dismisses critics, how lawsuits and insults target opponents, and how gun-policy changes overlap with family business interests.

What is “Trump Derangement Syndrome” and who uses it?

Trump Derangement Syndrome (TDS) is not a medical diagnosis. It is a political insult adapted from columnist Charles Krauthammer’s 2003 phrase “Bush Derangement Syndrome.” Writer Esther Goldberg first applied “Trump Derangement Syndrome” in The American Spectator in August 2015, initially against establishment Republicans who opposed Trump’s candidacy. The label soon spread to mean any strong criticism of Trump is irrational “derangement,” not legitimate dissent protected by the First Amendment.

Trump and his allies repeat the term on Truth Social, in rallies, and through the media network documented on this site. Spokespeople such as Steven Cheung have used it to bat away reporters’ questions. Fox hosts, OAN/Newsmax commentators, and podcast influencers in the “manosphere” echo it. The effect is to pathologize opponents while Trump retains maximum freedom to insult, threaten litigation, and command news cycles.

How does this conflict with the First Amendment?

The First Amendment protects speech against government punishment. It does not stop private insults, and it does not prevent someone from filing civil lawsuits (though many defamation suits fail). The tension is hypocrisy: Trump and allies invoke “free speech” for their own rhetoric while working to discredit, sue, or intimidate critics.

  • Defamation lawsuits against media, authors, and officials (often dismissed or settled); see Courts & delay
  • Public insults and nicknames aimed at journalists, judges, and witnesses; catalog at /trump-insults/
  • “Enemy of the people” rhetoric against press outlets, correlating with harassment of reporters
  • TDS labeling to avoid engaging with factual critiques of policy or conduct

Critics’ speech remains constitutionally protected whether or not Trump accepts it. Calling opponents “deranged” is itself protected speech, but using presidential power, litigation, and mob encouragement alongside that rhetoric raises separate abuse-of-power questions.

What did Trump say to militant groups and before January 6?

Sept. 29, 2020 debate: Asked to condemn white supremacists and militias, Trump instead said to the Proud Boys: “Proud Boys, stand back and stand by.” Members celebrated online as if they had received orders; the group was later prominent among those charged in Capitol-related violence. Trump later said he did not know much about the group.

Jan. 6, 2021 Ellipse speech: Trump told supporters to “fight like hell” and that “if you don’t fight like hell, you’re not going to have a country anymore,” then directed the crowd toward the Capitol during certification of the 2020 election. Hundreds of rioters, including Proud Boys and Oath Keepers leaders, were convicted of assaulting police and obstructing Congress. Trump was impeached (and acquitted) on incitement charges; he has called the speech “appropriate.”

What is the GrabAGun / mail-order gun proposal tied to Donald Trump Jr.?

In 2025 the ATF proposed rules (following a February executive order on Second Amendment access) that could allow licensed dealers to complete more firearm sales online and, in a related proposal, relax long-standing limits on mailing handguns to buyers’ homes. GrabAGun, an online retailer sometimes called “the Amazon of guns,” cited the proposal as a growth opportunity.

Donald Trump Jr. joined GrabAGun’s board and consulting team in late 2024, with SEC filings showing roughly 300,000 shares (~1% of the company). Reuters and the Washington Post reported the rule could benefit online sellers including GrabAGun; Trump Jr.’s spokesperson said he had “zero involvement” in drafting the ATF proposal. Ethics watchdogs flagged the overlap between expanded gun commerce and a presidential son’s equity stake.

How do the First and Second Amendments collide in this debate?

Trump and allies treat unrestricted political speech as a core right when it favors them, while TDS rhetoric treats opposing speech as mental illness. Separately, they treat firearm access as a Second Amendment priority, including proposals to ship guns through the mail to boost an online retailer linked to the president’s son.

Gun violence produces a different kind of “silencing”: CDC data document tens of thousands of firearm deaths annually in the United States (homicides, suicides, and accidents). Mass shootings and political violence have ended speech permanently for victims, journalists, and protesters. That is not a metaphor for hurt feelings; it is lethal force. Debates over “cancel culture” and TDS often ignore that expanded access to firearms has measurable body counts, while critics who only use words remain protected under the First Amendment.

This page does not argue for repealing the Second Amendment. It documents a pattern: maximal speech rights for the president and his allies, mockery or litigation for critics, and gun-policy shifts that coincide with family financial interest while real-world gun harm continues.

TLDR: free speech for me, “deranged” for thee

TDS reframes constitutional criticism as madness. Insults, lawsuits, and Jan. 6-era rally language pressure opponents while First Amendment protections still apply to dissent. Second-term gun rules could enrich a Trump Jr. stake in mail-order firearms even as gun deaths remain a leading cause of mortality. Words and bullets are not equivalent under the Constitution, but only one ends debate permanently.
